The company's development reﬂects a strategy of acquiring suitable oil and gas assets, improving production from existing ﬁelds and selectively pursuing new opportunities. ## iii. Overview of the Industry

 The upstream oil and gas industry covers the exploration, development and production of petroleum resources. It is diﬀerent from the midstream segment, which

focuses on transportation and storage, and the downstream segment, which covers reﬁning, distribution and retail. Upstream activities require substantial expenditure on licences, seismic studies, drilling, oﬀshore platforms, subsea equipment and production facilities. The industry is therefore capital-intensive and requires long-term planning. The industry also contains signiﬁcant uncertainty. A company cannot know with complete accuracy how much oil or gas can be recovered, how quickly a ﬁeld will produce or how future commodity prices will move. Revenue and proﬁtability may change because of ﬂuctuations in crude oil and gas prices, production disruptions, contractor costs, foreign-exchange rates and changes in reserve estimates. Oil and gas ﬁelds are depleting assets, so production will normally decline unless the operator carries out activities such as inﬁll drilling, workovers, enhanced recovery, new-ﬁeld development or asset acquisitions. In Malaysia, upstream activities are commonly conducted through Production Sharing Contracts administered by PETRONAS through Malaysia Petroleum Management. These contracts determine matters such as participating interests, work commitments, cost recovery, production sharing, reporting and decommissioning obligations. Operations in other countries are governed by the respective licences, concessions, tax regimes and regulatory authorities. Health, safety and environmental management are particularly important because oﬀshore operations involve risks such as equipment failure, ﬁre, spills, severe weather and workplace injury. Companies must also estimate and provide for the future cost of dismantling facilities and restoring sites. In addition, the energy transition has increased expectations concerning greenhouse-gas emissions, climate risk, environmental impact and sustainability disclosure. Therefore, the industry requires cooperation among ﬁnance, operations, engineering, legal, tax, risk and sustainability functions.

## Financial Accounting and Financial Reporting

 My ﬁnancial-accounting courses helped me understand the company's general-ledger and reporting processes. Transactions from diﬀerent business units must be recorded using the correct account code and reporting period before they can be summarised in ﬁnancial statements. During my placement, I prepared ﬁnancial working ﬁles for internal review,

supported Annual Financial Statements workings, and reviewed ledger extracts, transaction descriptions and supporting schedules. This showed me that the substance of a transaction is more important than its wording. For example, a payment may represent an operating expense, capital expenditure, prepayment, intercompany balance or another category depending on its purpose and period of beneﬁt. The concepts of double entry, accrual accounting and cut-oﬀ were directly applicable. An expense must be recognised in the correct period even when the invoice or payment occurs at a diﬀerent time. Items beneﬁting future periods may require prepayment treatment, while costs already incurred but not yet invoiced may require accruals. Incorrect timing or account classiﬁcation could aﬀect expenses, assets, liabilities, proﬁt and management reporting. My coursework therefore helped me understand why supporting documents, clear descriptions and review procedures are required before balances are ﬁnalised. For company-speciﬁc accounting treatments, I still needed guidance, and this improved through ﬁnancial statement preparation and audit support. 

## Bank Reconciliation and OCR Automation

 One of the most meaningful applications of my studies was bank reconciliation. A bank reconciliation compares the company's accounting records with bank information and explains diﬀerences such as timing items, unpresented payments, deposits in transit, bank charges, duplicated entries or transactions not yet recorded in the ledger. This task applies ﬁnancial accounting because the ﬁnal reconciled balance must agree with the amount reported as cash and bank balances. Monthly bank reconciliation was a core task. Statements arrived as PDFs in diﬀerent layouts from diﬀerent banks, so the work was heavily manual. I further developed the automation so that optical character recognition extracts dates, amounts, descriptions and references from those PDFs, compares them with the accounting records, separates matched items from exceptions, and produces an Excel output for review. This made unmatched transactions, possible duplicates and items requiring investigation easier to see. A practical issue I encountered was that the bank-reference ﬁeld shown in a bank statement could diﬀer from the reference extracted from another bank format. Therefore, matching could not depend on one ﬁeld alone. The automation had to consider a

combination of amount, date, transaction description and available reference information, while unusual items remained subject to manual review. This experience taught me that automation improves speed and consistency but does not remove the need for accounting judgement. It also showed the importance of data validation, exception reporting and maintaining an audit trail so that a reviewer can understand how each item was matched. This work was closely related to Accounting Information Systems and ﬁnancial data analytics. I learned how accounting controls can be incorporated into a technical workﬂow. For example, the program should not silently force a match when several transactions have the same amount. Instead, uncertain cases should be ﬂagged for review. The automation therefore supported, rather than replaced, the preparer and reviewer controls in the reconciliation process. Management Accounting and Expenditure Classiﬁcation Management-accounting concepts were applicable when I assisted with operating-expenditure working papers and ESG reporting information for submission to the Group. I reviewed transaction-level data and applied the required methodology to distinguish PMT and non-PMT items and to classify relevant transactions into categories such as recurring, non-recurring, intercompany and Z* items. The work required a consistent sequence of review because one classiﬁcation rule could take priority over another. This task demonstrated the importance of cost classiﬁcation. A transaction cannot be classiﬁed accurately only by looking at the account name; the description, counterparty, business purpose, supporting documentation and relationship with other group entities may also need to be considered. Consistent classiﬁcation makes management reports and sustainability expenditure reports more comparable between entities and reporting periods. I also learned that unexplained or missing classiﬁcations should be investigated instead of being assigned based on assumption.

## Financial Reporting Standards



The Group's FY2025 ﬁnancial statements state that they were prepared in accordance with Malaysian Financial Reporting Standards, International Financial Reporting Standards and the Companies Act 2016. The independent auditors reported that the ﬁnancial statements gave a true and fair view of the Group's and Company's ﬁnancial position as at 30 June 2025 and of their ﬁnancial performance and cash ﬂows for the year (Hibiscus Petroleum Berhad, 2025a). The Malaysian Accounting Standards Board conﬁrms that MFRSs are the oﬃcial accounting pronouncements applicable in Malaysia (MASB, 2026). Several standards are particularly relevant to the company. MFRS 6 applies to exploration and evaluation expenditure. The Group's accounting policy states that qualifying exploration costs are accumulated by area of interest, while costs relating to an abandoned area are written oﬀ. MFRS 116 and MFRS 136 are relevant to property, plant and equipment, depletion and impairment. MFRS 15 is relevant to oil and gas sales, for which revenue is generally recognised when control of the hydrocarbons is transferred to the customer. MFRS 137 is important for decommissioning provisions because the future cost of dismantling facilities and restoring sites is estimated, discounted and reviewed. Other relevant standards include MFRS 9 for ﬁnancial instruments, MFRS 16 for leases, MFRS 112 for taxation, MFRS 121 for foreign currency, and MFRS 10 and MFRS 11 for group and joint-arrangement reporting. 

## Companies Act, Bursa Malaysia and Corporate Governance

 The Companies Act 2016 requires proper accounting records and ﬁnancial statements that give a true and fair view. As a Main Market listed issuer, Hibiscus Petroleum is also subject to Bursa Malaysia's Main Market Listing Requirements, including periodic ﬁnancial reporting, disclosure of material information, annual-report requirements, related-party transaction rules and corporate-governance disclosure (Bursa Malaysia, 2026a). The annual report describes governance arrangements involving the Board, Board committees, management, internal control, internal audit and external audit. The controls I observed in the Financial Reporting department were consistent with these objectives. Transactions and balances were supported by detailed working papers, reconciliations and review. The bank-reconciliation automation also reﬂected compliance principles because it was designed to identify exceptions rather than conceal them. A

controlled automation should preserve source information, show the matching result and allow a reviewer to investigate unusual transactions. 

## Sustainability, Petroleum and Other Regulatory Requirements

 Bursa Malaysia requires listed issuers to include sustainability information in their annual reports and has enhanced the sustainability-reporting framework in alignment with IFRS Sustainability Disclosure Standards. These requirements increase the importance of reliable environmental, social and governance data and the connection between sustainability information and ﬁnancial reporting (Bursa Malaysia, 2024; 2026b). My involvement in ESG information for the Group showed that reported sustainability amounts must be traceable to transaction-level accounting records and classiﬁed using consistent criteria. The Group must also comply with its Production Sharing Contracts, licences and concessions in each operating jurisdiction. These may contain requirements relating to work programmes, cost recovery, production sharing, taxation, reporting and decommissioning. Health, safety and environmental laws are also signiﬁcant because non-compliance could result in operational disruption, penalties, remediation costs or reputational damage. In conclusion, compliance is not limited to preparing the annual report. It begins with accurate transaction recording, proper authorisation, consistent classiﬁcation, timely reconciliation, supporting documentation and eﬀective review throughout the year.
